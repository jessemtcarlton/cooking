## Temperature Control

### Why Temperature Matters

**Temperature is the #1 factor in cooking success.** Too hot = burned outside, raw inside. Too cold = steamed, gray, no crust.

### Essential Temperature Zones

#### Protein Cooking Temps (Internal)

**Beef & Lamb:**
- **120-125°F**: Rare (cool red center)
- **130-135°F**: Medium-Rare (warm red center) ⭐ BEST for steaks
- **135-145°F**: Medium (warm pink center)
- **145-155°F**: Medium-Well (slightly pink)
- **160°F+**: Well-Done (no pink, dry)

**Pork:**
- **145°F**: Modern safe temp (slightly pink, juicy) ⭐ RECOMMENDED
- **160°F**: Old guidelines (dry, overcooked)
- **195-205°F**: Pork shoulder for pulling (collagen breakdown)

**Poultry:**
- **165°F**: Chicken breast (minimum safe, pull at 160°F)
- **175-180°F**: Chicken thigh (higher temp = more tender)
- **165°F**: Turkey breast
- **180°F**: Turkey thigh/leg

**Fish & Seafood:**
- **110-120°F**: Tuna, swordfish (medium-rare)
- **120-130°F**: Salmon, most fish (medium, slightly translucent)
- **135-140°F**: Well-done fish (fully opaque)
- **120°F**: Scallops (opaque with translucent center)
- **140°F**: Shrimp (pink, opaque)
- **140-145°F**: Lobster (opaque, firm)

#### Cooking Method Temps

**Searing/Browning:**
- Pan surface: 400-500°F
- Oil shimmer point: 350-400°F
- Oil smoke point varies:
  - Butter: 350°F (burns easily)
  - Olive oil: 375-405°F
  - Vegetable/Canola: 400-450°F
  - Avocado oil: 520°F (best for high heat)
  - Grapeseed oil: 420°F

**Roasting:**
- Low roast: 250-300°F (gentle, even cooking)
- Medium roast: 350-375°F (standard)
- High roast: 425-450°F (browning, crisping)

**Baking:**
- Bread: 350-450°F (depending on type)
- Cakes: 325-350°F
- Cookies: 350-375°F
- Pastries: 375-425°F

**Frying:**
- Deep frying: 350-375°F (crispy, not greasy)
- Pan frying: 325-350°F
- Too low: Absorbs oil, greasy
- Too high: Burns outside, raw inside

### Carryover Cooking

🚨 **CRITICAL CONCEPT**: Meat continues cooking after removal from heat.

**How Much Carryover:**
- **Small cuts (steak, chops)**: 5-10°F rise
- **Large roasts (whole chicken, prime rib)**: 10-15°F rise
- **Very large (whole turkey)**: 15-20°F rise

**Example:**
- Want medium-rare steak (final: 135°F)
- Pull from heat at 125-130°F
- Rest 8-10 minutes
- Final temp: 135°F perfect

**Why It Happens:**
- Exterior is hotter than interior
- Heat migrates inward during rest
- Juices redistribute throughout meat

### Using a Thermometer Correctly

**Instant-Read Thermometer:**
1. Insert through SIDE of meat (not top)
2. Angle toward center/thickest part
3. Don't touch bone (reads hotter)
4. Don't touch pan (false reading)
5. Wait 2-3 seconds for accurate read
6. Check multiple spots for large roasts

**Probe/Leave-In Thermometer:**
- Insert before cooking
- Stays in during entire cook
- Set alarm for target temp
- Best for roasts, whole birds

### Temperature Troubleshooting

**"My thermometer gives different readings in different spots"**
- Normal! Meat cooks unevenly
- Take multiple readings
- Use lowest reading as your guide
- Thickest part is last to cook

**"My steak was 130°F but still looked rare"**
- Color isn't reliable (affected by age, pH, myoglobin)
- Trust the thermometer over color
- Grass-fed beef stays redder than grain-fed

## The Five Mother Sauces

French cuisine organizes sauces into five "mother sauces" - master these and you can make hundreds of derivative sauces.

### 1. Béchamel (White Sauce)

**Base:** Milk thickened with white roux
**Ratio:** 3 tbsp butter + 3 tbsp flour + 2 cups milk

**How to Make:**
1. Melt 3 tbsp (42g) butter in saucepan over medium heat
2. Add 3 tbsp (24g) flour, whisk constantly
3. Cook 2-3 minutes (white roux - no color)
4. Heat 2 cups (480ml) milk until warm
5. Add milk slowly, whisking constantly
6. Simmer 10-15 minutes until thick
7. Season with salt, white pepper, nutmeg

**Derivatives:**
- **Mornay**: Add 1 cup (100g) grated Gruyère cheese
- **Soubise**: Add 1 cup (150g) onion purée
- **Cream Sauce**: Replace milk with cream

**Uses:** Mac and cheese, lasagna, croque monsieur, vegetables gratin

**Detailed Recipe:** [→ Béchamel](./Mother-Sauces/Bechamel.md)

### 2. Velouté (Light Sauce)

**Base:** Light stock (chicken, fish, veal) thickened with blonde roux
**Ratio:** 3 tbsp butter + 3 tbsp flour + 2 cups stock

**How to Make:**
1. Melt 3 tbsp (42g) butter over medium heat
2. Add 3 tbsp (24g) flour, whisk
3. Cook 3-5 minutes (blonde roux - light gold)
4. Heat 2 cups (480ml) stock until warm
5. Add stock slowly, whisking
6. Simmer 20 minutes, skim impurities
7. Season with salt, white pepper

**Derivatives:**
- **Allemande**: Add egg yolk and cream
- **Suprême**: Made with chicken velouté + cream
- **Bercy**: Add shallots and white wine

**Uses:** Chicken dishes, fish, veal

**Detailed Recipe:** [→ Velouté](./Mother-Sauces/Veloute.md)

### 3. Espagnole (Brown Sauce)

**Base:** Brown stock thickened with brown roux, with tomatoes and mirepoix
**Ratio:** 3 tbsp butter + 3 tbsp flour + 2 cups brown stock + tomato paste

**How to Make:**
1. Make brown roux (cook until deep brown, 15-20 min)
2. Add 2 cups (480ml) brown stock slowly
3. Add 2 tbsp (30g) tomato paste
4. Add mirepoix (onion, carrot, celery)
5. Simmer 1-2 hours, skim frequently
6. Strain through fine sieve

**Derivatives:**
- **Demi-Glace**: Reduce espagnole with brown stock by half (ULTIMATE sauce base)
- **Bordelaise**: Demi-glace + red wine + shallots + bone marrow
- **Chasseur**: Add mushrooms, shallots, white wine, tomato

**Uses:** Beef, lamb, rich meat dishes

**Detailed Recipe:** [→ Espagnole](./Mother-Sauces/Espagnole.md)

### 4. Hollandaise (Butter Emulsion)

**Base:** Egg yolk emulsion with clarified butter and lemon
**Ratio:** 3 egg yolks + 1/2 cup (120ml) clarified butter + 1 tbsp lemon juice

**How to Make:**
1. Whisk 3 egg yolks + 1 tbsp (15ml) water in bowl
2. Place over double boiler (barely simmering water)
3. Whisk constantly until thickened (ribbon stage)
4. Remove from heat
5. Slowly drizzle in 1/2 cup (120ml) warm clarified butter
6. Whisk constantly (must emulsify)
7. Add 1 tbsp (15ml) lemon juice
8. Season with salt, cayenne

**Derivatives:**
- **Béarnaise**: Add tarragon, shallot reduction (for steak)
- **Choron**: Béarnaise + tomato paste
- **Maltaise**: Add orange juice and zest

**Uses:** Eggs Benedict, asparagus, fish, steak

**Detailed Recipe:** [→ Hollandaise](./Mother-Sauces/Hollandaise.md)

### 5. Tomato Sauce

**Base:** Tomatoes with aromatics
**Ratio:** 2 lbs (900g) tomatoes + aromatics

**How to Make:**
1. Sweat 1 diced onion, 2 garlic cloves in 2 tbsp (30ml) olive oil
2. Add 2 lbs (900g) crushed tomatoes
3. Add bouquet garni (bay, thyme, parsley)
4. Add pinch sugar (balances acid)
5. Simmer 45-60 minutes
6. Season with salt, pepper
7. Optional: Purée for smooth sauce

**Derivatives:**
- **Marinara**: Add oregano, basil, quick-cook (30 min)
- **Creole**: Add bell peppers, celery, Cajun spices
- **Spanish**: Add peppers, paprika

**Uses:** Pasta, pizza, shakshuka, braising

**Detailed Recipe:** [→ Tomato Sauce](./Mother-Sauces/Tomato-Sauce.md)

## Essential Techniques

### Roux (Thickening Agent)

**What it is:** Equal parts fat (butter) and flour, cooked together
**Why it works:** Flour starches gelatinize in fat, then thicken liquid

**Three Types:**

**White Roux** (2-3 minutes cooking):
- Color: Pale, no browning
- Flavor: Neutral, floury
- Uses: Béchamel, cream sauces
- Thickening power: STRONGEST

**Blonde Roux** (3-5 minutes):
- Color: Light golden
- Flavor: Slightly nutty
- Uses: Velouté
- Thickening power: MEDIUM

**Brown Roux** (15-20+ minutes):
- Color: Deep brown (like peanut butter)
- Flavor: Nutty, complex
- Uses: Gumbo, espagnole
- Thickening power: WEAKEST (but best flavor)

**How to Make Roux:**
1. Melt butter (or fat) over medium heat
2. Add equal amount flour by weight
   - 3 tbsp butter (42g) = 3 tbsp flour (24g)
   - NOT equal volume (flour weighs less)
3. Whisk constantly
4. Cook to desired color
5. 🚨 **WATCH CONSTANTLY** - can burn in seconds once dark

**Ratio for Sauce:**
- Thin sauce: 1 tbsp roux per 1 cup liquid
- Medium sauce: 2 tbsp roux per 1 cup liquid
- Thick sauce: 3 tbsp roux per 1 cup liquid

**Detailed Guide:** [→ Roux Techniques](./Techniques/Roux.md)

### Mirepoix (Aromatic Base)

**What it is:** Diced vegetables that form flavor base
**Classic French ratio:** 2 parts onion : 1 part carrot : 1 part celery

**How to Make:**
- Dice vegetables uniform size (1/2" for stocks, 1/4" for sauces)
- 2 cups (300g) onion
- 1 cup (150g) carrot
- 1 cup (150g) celery

**Cooking:**
- Sweat (cook without browning) for stocks
- Caramelize (brown) for richer flavor
- Cook in fat over medium heat 10-15 minutes

**Variations:**
- **Holy Trinity** (Cajun): Onion, celery, bell pepper
- **Soffritto** (Italian): Onion, carrot, celery (+ garlic, tomato)
- **Suppengrün** (German): Leek, carrot, celeriac

**Detailed Guide:** [→ Mirepoix](./Basic-Preparations/Mirepoix.md)

### Deglazing (Capturing Fond)

**What it is:** Adding liquid to hot pan to dissolve browned bits (fond)
**Why it matters:** Fond = concentrated flavor, foundation of pan sauces

**How to Deglaze:**
1. Cook protein (steak, chicken, etc.)
2. Remove protein, leave fond in pan
3. Pour off excess fat (leave 1-2 tbsp)
4. Return pan to heat
5. Add liquid (wine, stock, water)
6. 🚨 **May flame if alcohol** - pull from heat briefly
7. Scrape bottom with wooden spoon
8. Fond dissolves into liquid = instant sauce base

**Best Deglazing Liquids:**
- Red wine (beef, lamb)
- White wine (chicken, fish)
- Stock (any protein)
- Cognac/brandy (flambé, rich sauces)
- Vinegar (bright, acidic sauces)

**Detailed Guide:** [→ Deglazing](./Techniques/Deglazing.md)

### Reduction (Concentrating Flavor)

**What it is:** Simmering liquid to evaporate water, concentrate flavor
**Why it works:** Water evaporates, flavors and sugars concentrate

**How to Reduce:**
1. Bring liquid to simmer/boil
2. Don't cover (steam must escape)
3. Watch and stir occasionally
4. Reduce to desired consistency

**Reduction Stages:**
- **Nappe** (coats back of spoon): Light sauce consistency
- **Syrupy**: Thick like maple syrup
- **Glace**: Thick, glossy, concentrated

**Volume Guide:**
- "Reduce by half": 2 cups → 1 cup
- "Reduce to 1/4 cup": Original volume → 1/4 cup remains
- "Reduce to syrup": Very little liquid, coats pan

**🚨 Watch for Over-Reduction:**
- Can't add water back (dilutes flavor)
- Can go from perfect to burned in 30 seconds
- Better to under-reduce, then continue if needed

**Detailed Guide:** [→ Reduction](./Techniques/Reduction.md)

### Emulsion (Combining Fat and Water)

**What it is:** Forcing fat and water to combine (they naturally separate)
**Examples:** Mayonnaise, vinaigrette, hollandaise, butter sauces

**Two Types:**

**Temporary Emulsion:**
- Vinaigrette (oil + vinegar)
- Separates if left sitting
- Shake/whisk to re-combine

**Permanent Emulsion:**
- Mayonnaise, hollandaise
- Egg yolk acts as emulsifier
- Stays combined

**How to Emulsify:**
1. Start with emulsifier (egg yolk, mustard, etc.)
2. Add fat VERY slowly (drop by drop at first)
3. Whisk constantly
4. Once emulsion starts, can add faster (thin stream)
5. 🚨 **Too fast = broken sauce** (separated, greasy)

**Fixing Broken Emulsion:**
1. Start fresh with 1 egg yolk + 1 tsp water
2. Slowly whisk broken sauce into new yolk
3. Should re-emulsify

**Detailed Guide:** [→ Emulsions](./Techniques/Emulsions.md)

### Mounting (Monter au Beurre)

**What it is:** Whisking cold butter into sauce at end for richness and gloss
**Why it works:** Butter emulsifies into sauce, creates velvety texture

**How to Mount:**
1. Finish cooking sauce
2. Remove from heat (CRITICAL)
3. Reduce heat to LOW
4. Add cold butter cubes (1 tbsp at a time)
5. Whisk constantly as butter melts
6. Sauce becomes glossy, slightly thickened
7. 🚨 **Don't return to high heat** - emulsion breaks

**When to Mount:**
- Pan sauces after deglazing/reducing
- Compound sauces for finishing
- Any sauce that needs richness

**Butter Amount:**
- Light mounting: 1-2 tbsp per cup sauce
- Rich mounting: 3-4 tbsp per cup sauce

**Detailed Guide:** [→ Mounting Butter](./Techniques/Mounting-Butter.md)

## Seasoning Principles

### Salt

**Types:**
- **Kosher salt**: Best for cooking (easy to pinch, season)
  - Diamond Crystal: 1 tsp = 3g
  - Morton's: 1 tsp = 5g (denser)
- **Table salt**: Fine, very salty (1 tsp = 6g)
- **Sea salt**: Finishing, variable salinity
- **Fleur de sel**: Expensive finishing salt

**When to Salt:**

**Early (during cooking):**
- Seasons throughout
- Helps proteins retain moisture
- Draws out moisture (for browning)
- Examples: Salt steak 40 min before, salt pasta water

**Late (at end):**
- Adjusts final seasoning
- Doesn't affect texture
- Examples: Finishing salts on vegetables, final sauce adjustment

**How Much:**
- Pasta water: 1 tbsp (15g) per 4 quarts water (should taste like ocean)
- Steak: 1 tsp (3-5g) per side for 1" steak
- Vegetables: 1/4 tsp (1-2g) per pound
- Sauces: Season to taste at end

**🚨 Can't Un-Salt:**
- Add gradually
- Taste frequently
- Remember: Can always add more

### Acid

**Why it matters:** Brightens flavors, balances richness, cuts fat

**Types:**
- **Lemon juice**: Bright, clean (1-2 tbsp per sauce)
- **Vinegar**: Sharp, complex
  - Balsamic: Sweet, aged
  - Red wine: Rich, fruity
  - White wine: Clean, neutral
  - Sherry: Nutty, complex
- **Wine**: Adds acid + flavor
- **Tomato**: Acid + umami

**When to Add:**
- Early: Deglazing, marinades
- Late: Finishing, brightening

**How Much:**
- Start with 1 tsp (5ml)
- Add more gradually
- Should taste balanced, not sour

### Fat

**Why it matters:** Carries flavor, adds richness, creates mouthfeel

**Types:**
- **Butter**: Rich, creamy flavor (use at end, burns easily)
- **Olive oil**: Fruity, peppery (finishing, low-medium heat)
- **Neutral oils**: No flavor (high heat cooking)
- **Cream**: Rich, silky sauces

**Fat as Seasoning:**
- Finishing steak with butter
- Drizzling olive oil on pasta
- Mounting sauces with butter
- Swirling cream into soup

## Understanding Heat

### Direct vs Indirect Heat

**Direct Heat:**
- Food directly over heat source
- Grilling over flames
- Pan directly on burner
- **Uses:** Searing, browning, quick cooking

**Indirect Heat:**
- Food away from heat source
- Grilling on cool zone
- Oven roasting
- **Uses:** Gentle cooking, finishing, large roasts

### Dry vs Wet Heat

**Dry Heat Methods:**
- Roasting, baking, grilling, sautéing, frying
- Browning occurs (Maillard reaction)
- Creates crust, complex flavors
- Best for: Tender cuts, vegetables

**Wet Heat Methods:**
- Braising, stewing, poaching, steaming
- No browning (temperature too low)
- Tenderizes tough cuts
- Best for: Tough cuts, delicate proteins

### The Maillard Reaction

**What it is:** Chemical reaction between amino acids and sugars when heated
**Temperature:** Starts at 280°F (140°C), accelerates above 300°F (150°C)
**Result:** Browning, complex savory flavors

**Requirements:**
1. High heat (300°F+)
2. Dry surface (moisture prevents browning)
3. Time (needs to develop)

**Why It Matters:**
- Brown = flavor (not just appearance)
- Gray meat = no Maillard = bland
- This is why we sear steaks, brown chicken, toast bread

## Key Ratios to Memorize

**Roux:**
- Equal parts fat and flour by weight
- 2 tbsp roux thickens 1 cup liquid (medium thickness)

**Béchamel:**
- 3 tbsp butter + 3 tbsp flour + 2 cups milk

**Vinaigrette:**
- 3 parts oil : 1 part acid (vinegar/lemon)
- Add 1 tsp Dijon mustard to emulsify

**Stock Cooking:**
- Bones: 3-4 lbs per gallon water
- Mirepoix: 1 lb per gallon
- Simmer time: 4-6 hours (beef), 2-3 hours (chicken)

**Pasta:**
- 4 quarts water per 1 lb pasta
- 1 tbsp salt per 4 quarts water

**Rice:**
- 1 part rice : 2 parts liquid
- 1 cup rice = 3 cups cooked
